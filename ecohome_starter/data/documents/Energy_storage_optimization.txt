================================================================================
ENERGY STORAGE OPTIMIZATION
Sizing, Operating and Maintaining Battery and Thermal Storage
================================================================================
Category:  Energy Storage / Energy Efficiency
Audience:  Homeowners, small businesses, facility managers
Version:   October 2026

NOTE: Technology, pricing, incentives, codes and utility programs change
often. Figures here are typical ranges for planning only. Always check
manufacturer specifications and warranties. Battery installation involves
high-energy electrical equipment and must be done by qualified, licensed
professionals following local codes and permits.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT ENERGY STORAGE DOES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Energy storage saves energy for later use. It can:

  - Increase use of on-site solar by storing midday surplus for the evening
  - Shift consumption from expensive peak hours to cheaper hours
  - Reduce demand charges by trimming short power peaks
  - Provide backup power during outages
  - Support the grid through demand response programs or virtual power
    plants, where available

Storage does not create energy. Every conversion loses some, so storage is
worth it only when it provides value that outweighs those losses and costs:
bill savings, resilience, or grid benefits.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. TYPES OF STORAGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 Electrochemical batteries
  Lithium iron phosphate (LFP)
    - Long cycle life (often several thousand cycles), good thermal
      stability, tolerant of regular full charging. Widely used for stationary
      storage.
  Lithium nickel manganese cobalt (NMC)
    - Higher energy density. Often prefers moderate charge limits to
      maximize life.
  Lead-acid
    - Lower upfront cost per unit, but shorter life, lower usable depth of
      discharge, and more maintenance (for flooded types).
  Flow batteries
    - Long duration, scalable, long life; usually for larger commercial or
      utility projects.

2.2 Thermal storage
  - Hot water tanks: the most accessible form. A well-controlled electric or
    heat pump water heater stores energy as heat.
  - Ice storage or chilled water: used in commercial buildings to make ice or
    cold water at night or from surplus power and use it for cooling later.
  - Building thermal mass: concrete, masonry and water absorb and release
    heat. Pre-cooling or pre-heating uses the building itself as storage.
  - Phase change materials: store heat in the melting or freezing of materials.

2.3 Electric vehicles
  - EV batteries are large (often 50 to 100 kWh or more) compared with home
    batteries (often 10 to 15 kWh per unit). Smart charging can shift load.
    Vehicle-to-home (V2H) and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) can supply power if the
    vehicle, charger and utility support it.

2.4 Mechanical and utility-scale
  - Pumped hydro, compressed air and flywheels are used for grid-scale
    storage and are not discussed in detail here.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. KEY TERMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Energy capacity (kWh)       How much energy can be stored. Nominal vs usable
                              capacity differ; use usable.
  Power rating (kW)           How fast the system can charge or discharge.
                              Continuous and peak (surge) ratings differ.
  Round-trip efficiency       Energy out divided by energy in. Lithium-ion
                              systems are commonly in the range of about 85 to
                              95 percent for the battery system.
  Depth of discharge (DoD)    Share of capacity used in a cycle.
  State of charge (SoC)       Current charge level as a percentage.
  Cycle life                  Number of full charge-discharge cycles before
                              capacity falls to a stated level.
  Calendar aging              Capacity loss over time, even without cycling.
  C-rate                      Charge or discharge rate relative to capacity.
  Throughput warranty         Total energy (kWh or MWh) the warranty covers
                              over the battery's life.
  Self-consumption            Share of solar energy used on site.
  Self-sufficiency            Share of load met by solar plus storage.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. FIRST DECIDE THE GOAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Different goals lead to different sizes and settings.

  Goal                      Main design driver              Typical priority
  ------------------------  ------------------------------  -------------------
  Backup power              Critical load kWh and kW        Keep a high reserve
  Solar self-consumption    Evening use and surplus solar   Charge from solar
  Time-of-use savings       Price spread peak vs off-peak   Schedule by price
  Demand charge reduction   Peak kW and how short peaks are Limit peak demand
  Grid services             Program rules and availability  Follow program terms

A system serving several goals ("value stacking") may earn more, but settings
conflict. Keeping a large backup reserve, for example, reduces the energy
available for daily savings.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. SIZING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1 Sizing for backup
  1. List critical loads: refrigerator, lights, internet, medical devices,
     furnace blower, well pump and others.
  2. Estimate energy per day (kWh) and peak power (kW), including motor
     starting surge.
  3. Decide how many hours or days of backup you want.
  4. Convert to usable capacity, then to nominal capacity.

  Example
    Critical loads use about 6 kWh per day. You want 2 days of backup without
    solar recharge.
    Usable energy needed = 6 x 2 = 12 kWh.
    If the battery allows about 90 percent usable capacity,
    Nominal capacity = 12 / 0.9 = about 13.3 kWh.
    Check that the power rating covers the highest simultaneous load,
    including surges from motors and compressors.

  Solar recharge during an outage extends runtime. Note that whole-home
  backup needs much more capacity and power than critical-load backup.

5.2 Sizing for solar self-consumption
  - Look at hourly use after sunset and overnight, and the daily solar
    surplus. A battery slightly larger than the typical evening-to-morning
    use that solar could cover is usually the limit of useful size.
  - Extra capacity that never fills (or never discharges) adds cost without
    savings.
  - Seasonal variation matters: summer surplus may be large, while winter
    surplus may be too small to fill a large battery.

5.3 Sizing for time-of-use savings
  - Size to the amount of energy used during peak periods that you can
    reasonably shift.
  - Check that the battery can discharge for the whole peak window.

5.4 Sizing for demand charge reduction (commercial)
  - Use interval data (15-minute) to find peak events.
  - Measure how many kW you need to shave and for how long. A short, sharp
    peak is much easier and cheaper to shave than a long plateau.
  - Check whether demand charges use a monthly peak or a ratchet that holds
    the highest peak for many months.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. OPTIMIZING OPERATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.1 Time-of-use arbitrage
  The value of shifting energy is the price spread, after losses.

    Net gain per kWh charged = (Peak price x Round-trip efficiency) - Off-peak
                               price

  Example
    Off-peak price: $0.12 per kWh
    Peak price:     $0.36 per kWh
    Round-trip efficiency: 90 percent

    Net gain = (0.36 x 0.90) - 0.12 = 0.324 - 0.12 = $0.204 per kWh charged.

  If the spread is small, losses and wear can remove the benefit. Include
  battery degradation cost in your analysis.

6.2 Solar-first charging
  - Charge the battery from solar surplus first. Charging from the grid at
    peak prices is rarely beneficial.
  - Avoid exporting at low rates when you can store the energy and use it
    when it is worth more.

6.3 Reserve settings
  - Set a backup reserve (minimum SoC) based on your outage risk. A high
    reserve protects resilience but reduces daily savings.
  - Use storm or outage alerts: many systems can automatically raise the
    reserve and charge from the grid or solar before predicted severe
    weather.
  - Review the reserve seasonally. Lower it in mild seasons, raise it in
    storm or wildfire season.

6.4 Match charge and discharge to prices and loads
  - Discharge during the most expensive hours, or when household loads are
    highest, not at the first sign of evening.
  - Avoid discharging into low-value export hours.
  - Keep enough energy for the highest-price window, especially on cloudy
    days when solar may not refill the battery.

6.5 Load management with storage
  - Pair the battery with smart controls: delay EV charging, water heating
    and dryers so the battery does not cover large loads unnecessarily.
  - Avoid running large loads at the same time during an outage.

6.6 Demand response and virtual power plants
  - Some utilities pay customers for letting aggregated batteries support the
    grid during peak events.
  - Review limits on how often events occur, minimum reserve guarantees,
    and compensation terms before enrolling.

6.7 Review performance regularly
  - Compare actual savings with the forecast each quarter.
  - Adjust settings after rate changes, new loads (EV, heat pump) or
    changes in solar production.

7. BATTERY HEALTH AND LONGEVITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Temperature
    - Most lithium-ion batteries perform and age best at moderate
      temperatures, often about 59 to 77 F (15 to 25 C). High heat speeds
      aging, and cold reduces available power and capacity and can limit
      charging.
    - Install in a location that is sheltered and within the manufacturer's
      temperature range, in line with local codes.

  State of charge
    - NMC chemistries last longer if not held at 100 percent for long periods
      and not often fully emptied. LFP is more tolerant. Follow the
      manufacturer's guidance.
    - Avoid leaving a battery fully discharged for long periods.

  Cycling
    - Deep, frequent cycling uses up cycle life. Daily cycling is normal for
      solar systems, so choose a chemistry and warranty that suit it.

  Warranty
    - Check warranty length (often 10 years), minimum retained capacity at the
      end (often 60 to 70 percent), throughput limits, and operating
      conditions that void coverage.

  Software and monitoring
    - Keep firmware updated and monitor for faults, capacity trends and
      alerts. Capacity fade should be gradual. Rapid loss deserves a service
      check.

  Maintenance
    - Keep the area clear of storage, vegetation and debris. Follow the
      manufacturer's inspection schedule.

8. THERMAL STORAGE STRATEGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.1 Water heaters as thermal batteries
  - Heat water when electricity is cheap or when solar surplus is available.
    Heat pump water heaters make this efficient.
  - Raising the tank temperature above normal stores more energy but
    increases scald risk and standby loss, and it can reduce the efficiency
    of a heat pump water heater. If you store hotter water, use a
    thermostatic mixing valve to deliver safe temperatures at the tap.
  - Do not allow tank temperatures to stay low enough for bacterial growth
    such as Legionella. Follow plumbing code and manufacturer guidance.
  - Use controllers or built-in grid-connected modes for scheduling.

8.2 Building thermal mass
  - Pre-cool the building before peak hours, then let temperatures drift up
    slowly. Pre-heat in winter in the same way, within comfort limits.
  - Works best in buildings with good insulation and thermal mass, and with
    smart thermostats that coordinate with price signals.

8.3 Ice and chilled-water storage (commercial)
  - Chillers make ice or cold water at night when electricity is cheaper and
    air is cooler, and the building uses it during the day.
  - Reduces peak demand and may improve chiller efficiency at night.

9. INTEGRATION WITH SOLAR AND EVs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  AC-coupled systems
    - The battery has its own inverter and connects on the AC side. Easier
      to add to an existing solar system. Slightly lower round-trip
      efficiency because of extra conversions.

  DC-coupled systems
    - Solar and battery share a hybrid inverter. Often more efficient when
      charging from solar and can capture solar that would otherwise be
      clipped by the inverter.

  EV charging
    - Schedule charging for solar surplus or off-peak hours. Avoid charging
      during peak hours.
    - If the EV has V2H capability, check the warranty impact and the
      charger and transfer switch requirements.

  Smart panels and load controllers
    - Allow selective circuits to be powered during an outage and
      load-shed non-critical circuits automatically, extending backup time.

10. SAFETY, CODES AND INSTALLATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Use licensed installers and follow local permitting and inspection
    requirements.
  - Look for products tested and listed to recognized safety standards (for
    example, UL 9540 for energy storage systems, and UL 9540A test data for
    fire propagation).
  - Follow code requirements for location, spacing, ventilation, fire
    protection, and distance from living spaces and exits. Rules differ by
    jurisdiction and are updated frequently.
  - Install smoke and heat detection where required.
  - Do not stack items against a battery, block ventilation or modify
    enclosures.
  - Know how to shut down the system and who to call in an emergency.
  - Tell your insurer, and check whether your policy covers the equipment.
  - If a battery shows swelling, odor, smoke or unusual noise, move people
    away and contact emergency services.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. ECONOMICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Compare total value with total cost.

  Costs
    - Equipment, installation, permits, electrical upgrades
    - Maintenance, software fees, and eventual replacement
    - Financing costs

  Benefits
    - Bill savings from self-consumption, TOU shifting, and demand reduction
    - Payments from grid programs
    - Resilience value (avoided losses during outages, avoided generator
      costs, comfort and safety). This is real but hard to price. Decide how
      much it is worth to you before comparing payback.

  Tips
    - Run the numbers with your actual rate plan and hourly load data.
    - Check whether net metering or net billing changes make storage more
      valuable, such as low export rates.
    - Check incentives, but do not rely on programs that may change or end.
    - Compare cost per usable kWh and per kW, plus warranty terms.
    - Account for degradation: capacity declines over time.
    - Consider whether efficiency and demand reduction measures give a better
      return first.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. COMMERCIAL AND INSTITUTIONAL USE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Demand charge management is often the top value driver. Control logic
    must predict and respond to peaks quickly.
  - Combine storage with load control (HVAC pre-cooling, process scheduling)
    to reduce battery size.
  - Review rate structures for demand ratchets, standby charges, and rules
    on storage charging from the grid.
  - Consider microgrid designs for critical facilities such as clinics,
    shelters, data rooms and cold storage.
  - Plan for operations: staff training, remote monitoring, cybersecurity and
    maintenance contracts.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. END OF LIFE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Ask the manufacturer or installer about take-back and recycling.
  - Do not dispose of batteries in household trash.
  - Capacity falling to roughly 60 to 70 percent of original capacity is
    commonly treated as end of warranty life, but a battery can often still
    serve smaller roles.
  - Plan the replacement cost and downtime in your long-term budget.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Metric                          How to use it
  ------------------------------  ---------------------------------------------
  Measured round-trip efficiency  Compare with the datasheet; investigate drops

  Capacity retention              Compare usable kWh over time

  Self-sufficiency                Share of load met by solar and storage
  Bill savings vs baseline        Verify the investment case
  Peak demand reduction (kW)      For commercial demand charge strategies
  Backup reserve events           Track outages and how long backup lasted

16. COMMON MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Buying a large battery without a defined goal
  - Comparing nominal instead of usable capacity
  - Ignoring power (kW) limits, so the battery cannot start a well pump or
    air conditioner
  - Setting a high reserve all year and losing savings
  - Charging from the grid at peak prices
  - Installing in a space that gets too hot or too cold
  - Assuming whole-home backup from a small system
  - Overlooking warranty conditions and throughput limits
  - Not checking utility program rules before enrolling
  - Skipping efficiency upgrades that would shrink the system you need
  - Relying on payback only, without valuing resilience or considering
    degradation
