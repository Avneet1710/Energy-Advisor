================================================================================
HVAC OPTIMIZATION STRATEGIES
A Practical Guide to Reducing Heating and Cooling Energy Use
================================================================================
Category:  Energy Efficiency
Audience:  Homeowners, renters (where applicable), small-building managers
Version:   October 2026

NOTE: Savings figures in this document are typical ranges, not guarantees.
Actual results depend on climate, building condition, equipment, occupant
behavior and local energy prices. Work involving refrigerants, gas appliances
or electrical wiring should be done by licensed professionals.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHY HVAC MATTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) is usually the largest energy
end use in a building. In many homes it accounts for roughly 40 to 50 percent
of energy use, and in commercial buildings it often represents 30 to 50
percent. Small improvements in HVAC performance therefore have a larger effect
on the energy bill than almost any other upgrade.

The best results come from following this order:

  1. Reduce the load      (air sealing, insulation, windows, shading)
  2. Maintain equipment   (filters, coils, refrigerant charge, ducts)
  3. Control it smartly   (thermostat strategy, zoning, scheduling)
  4. Upgrade equipment    (right-sized, high-efficiency, often heat pumps)

Upgrading equipment before reducing the load often leads to oversized, more
expensive systems that run less efficiently.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. REDUCE THE LOAD FIRST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 Air sealing
  - Leaks around windows, doors, attic hatches, recessed lights, plumbing
    penetrations and the rim joist can account for a large share of heating
    and cooling loss.
  - Use caulk for gaps under 1/4 inch, expanding foam for larger gaps, and
    weatherstripping on moving parts such as doors and operable windows.
  - A blower-door test (offered by energy auditors) shows where air leaks.

2.2 Insulation
  - Attics are usually the most cost-effective place to add insulation.
  - Check recommended R-values for your climate zone and compare them with
    what you have. Compressed or uneven insulation performs below its rating.
  - Insulate and air-seal together. Insulation slows heat flow but does not
    stop air movement.

2.3 Windows and shading
  - Low-emissivity (low-e) coatings reduce heat transfer.
  - In cooling climates, exterior shading (awnings, trees, solar screens)
    blocks heat better than interior blinds because heat is stopped before it
    enters the home.
  - In heating climates, keep south-facing windows unobstructed in winter to
    gain free solar heat.
  - Insulated cellular shades and heavy curtains reduce winter heat loss at
    night.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. RIGHT-SIZING EQUIPMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Oversized equipment is one of the most common HVAC problems. An oversized air
conditioner cools the space quickly, then shuts off before it has removed
enough moisture. This is called short cycling. It causes uneven temperatures,
humidity problems, higher wear and lower efficiency.

Best practice:
  - Ask contractors to perform a load calculation (Manual J in North America)
    instead of sizing by square footage or by copying the old system.
  - Ask for duct design calculations (Manual D) and equipment selection
    (Manual S) for new or replaced systems.
  - After air sealing and insulation improvements, the calculated load is
    often lower than expected, so calculate after those upgrades when
    possible.
  - Variable-speed and inverter-driven equipment can modulate output and
    tolerates sizing differences better than single-speed equipment.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. EQUIPMENT TYPES AND EFFICIENCY RATINGS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1 Common efficiency metrics (higher is better, except where noted)

  SEER2   Seasonal cooling efficiency of air conditioners and heat pumps.
  EER2    Cooling efficiency at a fixed hot-weather test condition.
  HSPF2   Seasonal heating efficiency of air-source heat pumps.
  COP     Units of heat delivered per unit of electricity used.
  AFUE    Fuel efficiency of furnaces and boilers, as a percentage.

Compare products using the same metric. Older ratings (SEER, HSPF) and newer
ones (SEER2, HSPF2) use different test methods and are not directly equal.

4.2 Heat pumps
  - Heat pumps move heat instead of creating it. They typically deliver two to
    four units of heating for each unit of electricity, so they can use far
    less energy than electric resistance heating.
  - Cold-climate models maintain capacity at low outdoor temperatures. Check
    the manufacturer's capacity data at your design temperature.
  - Ductless mini-splits suit homes without ducts, additions and
    zone-by-zone upgrades.
  - Dual-fuel systems pair a heat pump with a gas furnace and switch based on
    outdoor temperature or energy price.

4.3 Furnaces and boilers
  - Condensing furnaces and boilers (typically 90 percent AFUE or higher)
    extract more heat from exhaust gases than non-condensing models.
  - Modulating burners and variable-speed blowers improve comfort and reduce
    energy use.

4.4 Central air conditioners
  - Variable-speed compressors and blowers run longer at lower output, which
    improves humidity control and efficiency.
  - A properly matched indoor coil and outdoor unit is required to achieve
    the rated efficiency.

4.5 Geothermal (ground-source) heat pumps
  - Exchange heat with the ground, which stays at a stable temperature.
  - Higher installation cost, but high efficiency and long equipment life.

4.6 Replacement timing
  - Consider replacement when equipment is near the end of its typical life
    (often 12 to 20 years for central air conditioners and heat pumps, longer
    for some furnaces), repairs are frequent, or the refrigerant is being
    phased out.
  - Planned replacement allows time to compare quotes and incentives.
    Emergency replacement usually limits choices.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. DUCTWORK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Ducts in attics, crawl spaces and garages can lose a substantial share of
conditioned air through leaks and conduction. Commonly cited estimates place
typical losses at 20 to 30 percent of airflow in leaky systems.

Actions:
  - Seal duct joints and seams with mastic sealant or approved foil tape.
    Standard cloth "duct tape" fails over time.
  - Insulate ducts that pass through unconditioned spaces.
  - Seal the boot connections where ducts meet floors, walls and ceilings.
  - Make sure each room has an adequate return air path. Closed doors can
    starve a room of return air; use transfer grilles or jump ducts.
  - Avoid closing too many supply registers. It raises duct pressure, increases
    leakage and can reduce system efficiency.
  - Request a duct leakage test when replacing equipment or after sealing work.
  - If ducts are in poor condition or poorly routed, consider moving them
    inside the conditioned envelope or switching to ductless systems.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. MAINTENANCE SCHEDULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Regular maintenance protects efficiency, comfort and equipment life.

  Monthly (during heavy use)
    - Check the air filter; replace or clean when dirty.
    - Make sure supply and return vents are not blocked by furniture.

  Quarterly
    - Check the condensate drain for clogs.
    - Keep the outdoor unit clear of leaves, grass and debris, with about
      two feet of clearance on all sides.

  Annually (before each cooling or heating season)
    - Have a technician check refrigerant charge, airflow, electrical
      connections, blower and burner or heat strip operation.
    - Clean indoor and outdoor coils.
    - Test safety controls and, for combustion equipment, check venting and
      carbon monoxide safety.

Filter guidance:
  - A clogged filter restricts airflow and can raise energy use noticeably;
    replacing a dirty filter is often cited as saving 5 to 15 percent of the
    system's energy use.
  - Filters are rated by MERV. MERV 8 to 13 suits most residential systems.
    Very high MERV filters can restrict airflow in systems not designed for
    them, so check the equipment manufacturer's guidance.

Refrigerant:
  - Low or high refrigerant charge reduces efficiency and shortens
    compressor life. Only licensed technicians should check or adjust it.
  - A system that repeatedly needs refrigerant has a leak that must be found
    and repaired.

7. THERMOSTAT AND SCHEDULING STRATEGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7.1 Recommended starting setpoints
  - Heating: about 68 F (20 C) when home and awake.
  - Cooling: about 78 F (26 C) when home.
  - Adjust for health, age, and personal comfort. Efficiency is a trade-off,
    not a rule.

7.2 Setbacks
  - Lowering the heating setpoint by 7 to 10 F (4 to 6 C) for about eight
    hours a day, such as while sleeping or away, can reduce annual heating
    and cooling costs by up to around 10 percent.
  - Raise the cooling setpoint when the home is empty, but avoid extreme
    settings in very humid climates where mold and moisture are a concern.

7.3 Heat pump considerations
  - Large setbacks can cause a heat pump to call for electric resistance
    backup heat when it recovers, wiping out the savings.
  - Use a thermostat that supports heat pumps, limit recovery to gradual
    steps (for example 2 F at a time), and set the lockout temperature for
    backup heat as low as comfort allows.

7.4 Control tips
  - Use fan setting "auto" instead of "on" so the blower does not run
    continuously, except where continuous low-speed circulation is
    intentional and uses an efficient (ECM or variable-speed) motor.
  - Avoid placing the thermostat in direct sun, near vents or near heat
    sources.
  - Ceiling fans cool people, not rooms. Turn them off when no one is present.
    In summer, run them to push air down; in winter, run on low speed in the
    opposite direction to circulate warm air.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. ZONING AND VENTILATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.1 Zoning
  - Dividing a building into zones lets you condition only occupied areas.
  - Options include motorized duct dampers, multiple thermostats with
    separate systems, and multi-zone ductless units.
  - Zoning works best when the equipment can modulate. Single-speed systems
    with poorly designed dampers can create excess pressure and noise.

8.2 Ventilation
  - Tight buildings need controlled ventilation for indoor air quality.
  - Heat recovery ventilators (HRV) and energy recovery ventilators (ERV)
    bring in fresh air while recovering much of the heat (and, for ERVs,
    moisture) from outgoing air.
  - Use bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans as needed, and run them only as
    long as necessary. Make sure they vent outdoors, not into the attic.

8.3 Humidity
  - Aim for indoor relative humidity of roughly 30 to 50 percent.
  - In humid climates, a dehumidifier or a variable-speed air conditioner
    with humidity control allows a higher cooling setpoint while staying
    comfortable.
  - In dry winter climates, over-humidifying causes window condensation and
    can increase heat loss and moisture damage.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. COMMERCIAL AND LARGE-BUILDING STRATEGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9.1 Controls and scheduling
  - Use a building automation system (BAS) to schedule start and stop times
    by occupancy, not by habit.
  - Use optimal-start strategies so spaces reach setpoint just as occupants
    arrive.
  - Review schedules at least quarterly. Equipment running on weekends or
    after hours is one of the most common wastes.

9.2 Reset strategies
  - Supply air temperature reset: raise cooling supply temperature when load
    is light.
  - Chilled water and hot water temperature reset based on load or outdoor
    temperature.
  - Static pressure reset: lower fan duct pressure until the most demanding
    zone is just satisfied.

9.3 Variable-speed drives
  - Fan and pump power falls roughly with the cube of speed. Slowing a fan to
    80 percent of full speed reduces its power draw to about half.
  - Variable-frequency drives (VFDs) on fans and pumps are among the most
    cost-effective commercial upgrades.

9.4 Economizers and demand-controlled ventilation
  - Air-side economizers use cool outdoor air to cool the building when
    conditions allow. Check that dampers, sensors and actuators work, because
    stuck dampers are common.
  - Demand-controlled ventilation uses CO2 sensors to adjust outdoor air to
    actual occupancy.

9.5 Retro-commissioning
  - Systematic testing and tuning of existing systems often finds savings in
    the range of 5 to 15 percent of building energy use, mostly through
    controls corrections with low or no capital cost.

10. TROUBLESHOOTING GUIDE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Symptom                         Common causes to check
  ------------------------------  ---------------------------------------------
  Rising bills, same usage        Dirty filter or coils, low refrigerant,
                                  duct leaks, failing components
  Rooms too hot or cold           Unbalanced airflow, blocked returns, leaky
                                  or undersized ducts, poor insulation
  System short cycles             Oversizing, dirty filter, thermostat
                                  placement, refrigerant problems
  High indoor humidity            Oversized AC, fan left on, leaky ducts,
                                  low airflow, ventilation issues
  Frost or ice on outdoor coil    Normal brief defrost for heat pumps; if
                                  excessive, airflow or defrost control issues
  Heat pump uses backup heat      Thermostat settings, aggressive setbacks,
  too often                       outdoor sensor problem, undersized system
  Unusual noise or smell          Loose parts, failing motor, dirty burners,
                                  moisture; shut off and call a professional

If you smell gas or suspect carbon monoxide, leave the building and contact
emergency services.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. PRIORITIZED ACTION PLAN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Low cost, do now
  [ ] Replace or clean filters on schedule
  [ ] Clear vents, returns and the outdoor unit
  [ ] Set a schedule or setbacks on the thermostat
  [ ] Weatherstrip doors and caulk visible gaps
  [ ] Close drapes on sunny summer windows; open them on sunny winter days

Moderate cost
  [ ] Book annual professional tune-up
  [ ] Seal and insulate accessible ducts
  [ ] Add attic insulation and seal attic air leaks
  [ ] Install a smart or programmable thermostat compatible with your system
  [ ] Install exterior shading on the sunniest windows

Larger investments
  [ ] Commission a professional energy audit with blower-door test
  [ ] Replace aging equipment with right-sized high-efficiency units or heat
      pumps based on a load calculation
  [ ] Add zoning or ductless units for problem areas
  [ ] Add ventilation with heat or energy recovery to tight homes


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. MEASURING RESULTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Compare monthly energy use against heating and cooling degree days, not
    just against last year's bill. Weather is the biggest swing factor.
  - Track kWh per square foot per year (or energy use intensity in
    commercial buildings) and compare with similar buildings.
  - Record before and after readings, such as run time, temperatures and
    utility meter readings, when making a change.
  - Use utility data portals, smart meter data or a whole-home monitor to
    see daily and hourly patterns.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Replacing equipment with the same size without a load calculation
  - Upgrading equipment while leaving leaky ducts and attics unaddressed
  - Setting the thermostat far below or above the target to "speed up"
    heating or cooling (it does not work faster and often overshoots)
  - Closing vents in unused rooms without checking system pressure
  - Skipping filter changes during peak season
  - Buying the highest-MERV filter without checking system compatibility
  - Choosing the cheapest bid without comparing the scope of work, warranty
    and commissioning steps
