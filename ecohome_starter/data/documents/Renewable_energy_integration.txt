================================================================================
RENEWABLE ENERGY INTEGRATION
Planning, Sizing and Operating On-Site Renewable Energy Systems
================================================================================
Category:  Renewable Energy / Energy Efficiency
Audience:  Homeowners, small businesses, facility managers
Version:   October 2026

NOTE: Costs, incentives, interconnection rules and net metering policies vary
widely by country, state and utility, and they change frequently. Verify
current programs with your utility, local government and a licensed installer
before making a decision. Electrical and structural work must be done by
qualified, licensed professionals.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. OVERVIEW AND STRATEGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Renewable energy works best as the last step in an efficiency-first plan:

  1. Reduce consumption (insulation, efficient HVAC, LED lighting, controls)
  2. Electrify strategically (heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, EVs)
  3. Generate on site (solar PV is the most common option)
  4. Store and shift (batteries, thermal storage, smart controls)
  5. Procure clean energy for what remains (community solar, green tariffs)

Reducing the load first means a smaller, cheaper renewable system is needed,
and avoids paying to generate energy that would have been wasted.

Solar PV is the most widely applicable. Small wind rarely performs well in
typical residential sites because turbulence near buildings and trees reduces
output, so confirm the local wind resource before buying.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. SITE ASSESSMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 Solar resource
  - In the northern hemisphere, south-facing roofs usually produce the most
    energy over a year. East and west faces produce somewhat less in total
    but can better match morning and late afternoon demand.
  - Optimal tilt is often close to the site's latitude, but moderate
    differences have a small effect on annual output.
  - Shade from trees, chimneys, vents and neighboring buildings can reduce
    output sharply. Even partial shade on one panel can affect a whole string
    unless the system uses module-level electronics.

3.2 Roof and structure
  - Roof condition: if the roof will need replacement within about 10 years,
    consider replacing it before installing solar.
  - Confirm structural capacity and roofing material with the installer.
  - Check fire-code setbacks, access pathways and local rules for roof
    layouts.

3.3 Electrical service
  - Check main panel capacity, breaker space and busbar rating. Backfeeding
    solar power into a panel is limited by code, and a panel upgrade may be
    needed.
  - Identify where the inverter, disconnects and meters will be located.

3.4 Regulatory and community factors
  - Permits, inspections and utility interconnection approvals are normally
    required.
  - Homeowner associations (HOAs) and local ordinances may regulate placement
    or appearance; many jurisdictions limit how far an HOA can restrict solar.
  - Renters and condominium owners can look at community solar or shared
    systems.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. SIZING A SOLAR PV SYSTEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1 Method
  1. Find annual electricity use in kWh from 12 months of bills.
  2. Look up the local average peak sun hours per day (a measure of solar
     resource; many locations fall between 3.5 and 6).
  3. Apply a system derate factor of about 0.75 to 0.85 to account for
     inverter losses, wiring, temperature, soiling and shading.
  4. Calculate system size:

       System size (kW) = Annual use (kWh) / (Peak sun hours x 365 x Derate)

4.2 Worked example
  Annual use:        9,000 kWh
  Peak sun hours:    4.5 per day
  Derate factor:     0.80

  System size = 9,000 / (4.5 x 365 x 0.80) = 9,000 / 1,314 = about 6.8 kW

  This would cover roughly 100 percent of annual use on paper. Real output
  varies by year and by month.

4.3 Sizing considerations
  - Planned electrification (EV, heat pump) raises future use. Size for
    expected use, not only the past, if you are certain of the changes.
  - Some utilities limit system size to a percentage of past usage or cap
    exports. Check local rules.
  - Over-sizing is not always better. If excess generation is credited at a
    low export rate, a smaller system with higher self-consumption may have
    better economics.
  - Output degrades slowly. A common planning figure is about 0.5 percent
    per year.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. SYSTEM COMPONENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Solar modules
    - Compare efficiency, temperature coefficient, warranty (often 25 years
      for product and performance), and manufacturer reputation.

  Inverters
    - String inverter: lowest cost, one conversion point; works best with
      uniform, unshaded arrays.
    - Microinverters: one per panel; better for shaded or complex roofs and
      provide module-level monitoring.
    - String inverter with power optimizers: module-level control with a
      central inverter.
    - Hybrid inverter: manages solar and battery together.
    - Inverters usually need replacement once during the life of the array,
      often after 10 to 15 years, unless a long warranty applies.

  Racking and mounting
    - Must match the roof type and wind and snow loads. Flashing and
      penetrations should be done by experienced roofers or installers.

  Monitoring
    - Panel-level or system-level monitoring shows production and flags
      failures. Make sure you can access data without a service fee, or know
      the fee in advance.

  Safety equipment
    - Rapid shutdown devices, disconnects and labeling required by code.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. GRID INTERACTION AND BILLING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Compensation rules strongly affect system design.

  Net metering
    - Exported energy earns credits, often at or near the retail rate, which
      offset later consumption. Rules about credit rollover and fees vary.

  Net billing / export rates
    - Exports are credited at a lower rate (often related to wholesale or
      avoided cost), so using solar on site is worth more than exporting.

  Time-of-use (TOU) plans
    - Value depends on when you generate and consume. Solar produces most at
      midday, while TOU peaks often occur in late afternoon and evening.

  Fixed charges and demand charges
    - These reduce the savings achievable from solar alone, especially for
      commercial customers.

  Interconnection
    - Application, utility review, inspection and permission to operate are
      required before switching the system on. Do not energize without
      permission.

Questions to ask the utility
  - What are the export compensation rules, and how long are they guaranteed?
  - Are there size limits or fees?
  - How long is the interconnection process?
  - Does the rate plan change if I add solar, EVs or storage?


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. MATCHING DEMAND TO GENERATION (SELF-CONSUMPTION)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Using solar power directly is usually more valuable than exporting it.

  Self-consumption rate = solar energy used on site / solar energy produced
  Self-sufficiency      = share of total consumption met by solar (and
                          storage)

Ways to increase self-consumption
  - Shift flexible loads to midday: dishwasher, laundry, pool pump, water
    heating, EV charging, battery charging.
  - Pre-cool or pre-heat the home using solar surplus, within comfort limits.
  - Use a heat pump water heater as a thermal battery by scheduling heating
    during peak solar output.
  - Install a battery to move surplus daytime output to the evening (see the
    energy storage guide).
  - Use smart controllers or solar diverters to send surplus energy to water
    heating when exports are low-value.
  - Use home automation to start loads when production exceeds a threshold.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. OTHER RENEWABLE AND LOW-CARBON OPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8.1 Solar water heating
  - Collectors heat water or a heat-transfer fluid that preheats domestic
    hot water. Works best with steady hot water use. Needs freeze protection
    in cold climates. In many areas, a heat pump water heater plus solar PV
    offers similar benefits with simpler equipment.

8.2 Ground-source heat pumps
  - Use stable ground temperatures for efficient heating and cooling. Good
    for new construction or when existing ducts, land and budget allow.

8.3 Small wind
  - Requires a strong wind resource and tall towers clear of obstructions.
    Obtain site wind data and local permits before investing.

8.4 Community solar, green tariffs and power purchase agreements
  - These options provide renewable energy without rooftop equipment.
  - Check contract term, pricing structure, cancellation fees, and whether
    renewable energy certificates are included and verified.

8.5 Biomass and micro-hydro
  - Specialized options for suitable sites. Consider emissions, fuel supply
    and permitting.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. ELECTRIFICATION SYNERGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Heat pumps for space and water heating convert electricity to heat
    efficiently, so renewable electricity can displace fossil fuels directly.
  - Electric vehicles can charge from midday solar. Smart chargers can
    follow solar production or off-peak rates.
  - Induction cooktops are efficient and can be powered by solar.
  - Plan electrical capacity (panel size, circuits) for planned electrification
    before choosing system size and equipment.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. COMMERCIAL AND INSTITUTIONAL CONSIDERATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Rooftop and carport solar can reduce energy charges and, with
    storage or load control, demand charges.
  - Analyze 15-minute or hourly interval data to size systems and understand
    peak demand.
  - Financing options include purchase, loan, lease and power purchase
    agreement (PPA). Compare ownership benefits, risk, and incentives.
  - Microgrids with storage and controls can provide resilience for critical
    facilities.
  - Coordinate with energy efficiency, since reducing HVAC and lighting load
    shrinks the required solar size.
  - Check roof warranty and structural requirements, and coordinate roof
    replacement timing.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. FINANCIAL EVALUATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Simple payback = Net system cost / Annual savings

  Better measures
    - Lifetime savings and net present value
    - Levelized cost of energy (cost per kWh over the system's life) compared
      with utility rates, which tend to rise over time but not predictably

Include
  - Equipment, installation, permits, electrical upgrades, roof work
  - Incentives: tax credits, rebates, performance payments, and
    local programs. Programs change, so verify eligibility, deadlines and
    required paperwork before relying on them.
  - Financing costs and fees
  - Inverter replacement and insurance
  - Degradation of about 0.5 percent per year
  - Changes in electricity rates and net metering rules
  - Impact on property value and resale (check ownership, leases and PPAs
    that can complicate a home sale)

Get at least three quotes, and compare equipment, warranties, production
estimates, and installer licensing and references, not only price.

12. INSTALLATION AND COMMISSIONING CHECKLIST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  [ ] Contract lists equipment models, system size, production estimate,
      timeline and warranty terms
  [ ] Permits obtained; utility interconnection application filed
  [ ] Roof penetrations flashed and sealed; roofer warranty confirmed
  [ ] Inspection passed; permission to operate received
  [ ] Monitoring set up and verified
  [ ] Production compared with the estimate over the first months
  [ ] As-built drawings, manuals, warranty documents and shutdown
      instructions stored
  [ ] Labels and shutdown procedures understood by the owner


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. OPERATIONS AND MAINTENANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Check monitoring monthly. Compare output with similar weeks in the past
    and with the expected curve.
  - Clean panels only if soiling is significant, such as heavy dust, pollen or
    bird droppings. Follow the manufacturer's guidance and avoid
    unsafe roof access. In many climates, rain is enough.
  - Trim vegetation that starts to shade the array.
  - Inspect visible wiring, mounts and roof flashing periodically.
  - Clear snow only if it is safe to do so.
  - Investigate sudden production drops: inverter fault, tripped breaker,
    module failure, new shading or monitoring outage.
  - Keep the inverter's ventilation area clear.

Performance ratio (actual output compared with expected output for measured
sunlight) is a useful health indicator. Persistent decline of more than a
few percent warrants an inspection.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
14. SAFETY AND RESILIENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Standard grid-tied solar systems shut down during a grid outage for
    safety. Backup power requires a battery or generator system designed for
    islanding.
  - Rapid shutdown, labeling and access pathways support firefighter safety.
  - Do not work on DC wiring; panels produce voltage whenever there is light.
  - Use licensed installers and obtain inspections.
  - Keep documentation accessible to emergency responders where required.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
15. PLANNING TIMELINE (TYPICAL)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Weeks 1-2    Collect bills; get an energy audit; fix cheap efficiency issues
  Weeks 2-4    Request multiple quotes; check roof and panel
  Weeks 4-6    Choose installer; sign contract; finalize equipment
  Weeks 6-12   Permits and utility application
  Weeks 12-16  Installation, inspection, permission to operate
  Month 4+     Verify production; adjust loads and rates

Timelines vary by region and utility. Start well before an incentive deadline
if one applies.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
16. COMMON MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Installing solar before addressing high-impact efficiency upgrades
  - Ignoring shading or using optimistic production estimates
  - Sizing based on rules of thumb without checking export rates
  - Skipping a roof assessment
  - Signing long contracts or leases without understanding escalators, transfer
    terms and fees
  - Assuming incentives or net metering rules will not change
  - Forgetting that a grid-tied system will not power the home in an outage
  - Not monitoring the system after installation
