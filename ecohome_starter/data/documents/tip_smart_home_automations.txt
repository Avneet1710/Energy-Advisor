================================================================================
SMART HOME AUTOMATION TIPS
Using Sensors, Schedules and Controls to Cut Energy Waste
================================================================================
Category:  Energy Efficiency / Home Automation
Audience:  Homeowners and renters
Version:   October 2026

NOTE: Savings figures are typical ranges and vary by household. Smart devices
use some energy themselves, so choose devices that replace real waste. Work on
household wiring should be done by a qualified electrician.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. GUIDING PRINCIPLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Measure first. Know which loads use the most energy before buying
     anything.
  2. Automate the big loads: heating and cooling, water heating, EV charging,
     pool pumps and large appliances.
  3. Remove waste that no one notices: standby power, lights left on, heating
     empty rooms.
  4. Keep comfort and safety first. An automation that annoys the household
     gets disabled and saves nothing.
  5. Prefer simple, reliable rules over complex ones. Every extra rule is
     something that can break or conflict.
  6. Fail safe. If the internet, hub or device goes offline, the home should
     fall back to a comfortable, safe state.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. SMART THERMOSTATS AND CLIMATE CONTROL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Heating and cooling is the largest opportunity (see the HVAC guide).

Features that save energy
  - Scheduling: lower heating and raise cooling setpoints when the home is
    asleep or empty.
  - Geofencing: use phone location to detect when everyone has left and when
    someone is returning.
  - Occupancy sensing: detects presence in the home.
  - Remote room sensors: average temperatures across rooms or prioritize the
    rooms in use.
  - Learning modes: build a schedule from your manual adjustments. Review the
    learned schedule, because it can lock in wasteful habits.
  - Energy reports: show run time and highlight unusual behavior.

Tips
  - Check compatibility before buying: wire types (C-wire availability),
    heat pump support, multi-stage and variable-speed equipment, and
    radiant or line-voltage systems.
  - For heat pumps, use settings that avoid triggering electric backup heat
    during recovery from setbacks.
  - Use vacation mode for extended absences, with a protective minimum
    temperature to avoid frozen pipes or excess humidity.
  - Link window or door contact sensors so heating or cooling pauses when a
    window is open.
  - Set humidity limits as well as temperature limits in humid climates.
  - Typical savings from thermostat scheduling are on the order of 8 to 10
    percent of heating and cooling energy when the home is regularly
    unoccupied or when setbacks are used consistently. A home that already
    uses setbacks will save less.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. LIGHTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Step 1: Switch to LED. LED bulbs use at least 75 percent less energy than
incandescent bulbs and last many times longer. This is usually the cheapest
upgrade.

Step 2: Control when lights are on.
  - Vacancy sensors turn lights off when a room is empty (best for closets,
    bathrooms, garages, laundry rooms and offices).
  - Occupancy sensors turn lights on and off automatically. Use manual-on
    (vacancy) mode where automatic-on is not needed.
  - Timers and sunrise/sunset schedules for exterior lighting.
  - Daylight sensors dim or turn off lights when natural light is available.
  - Dimming and scenes reduce power use and improve comfort.

Tips
  - Use "all off" scenes at bedtime and when leaving.
  - Choose dimmers that are compatible with LED bulbs to avoid flicker.
  - Keep smart bulbs powered at the fixture. A smart bulb behind a wall switch
    that is turned off cannot be automated; consider smart switches instead.
  - Smart bulbs draw a small amount of power while idle. Use smart switches
    for fixtures with many bulbs.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. STANDBY POWER AND SMART PLUGS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Many devices draw power even when "off." Standby loads are commonly estimated
at roughly 5 to 10 percent of household electricity use, depending on the
number of electronics.

Good candidates for smart plugs or smart power strips
  - Entertainment centers (TV, game console, soundbar, streaming box)
  - Home office equipment (monitors, printers, chargers, speakers)
  - Seldom-used appliances (secondary refrigerators or freezers are a
    different case; see below)
  - Chargers and power adapters

Devices to leave powered or treat carefully
  - Refrigerators and freezers (unless intentionally managing a spare unit)
  - Modems, routers and hubs that run the automation system
  - Medical devices and security systems
  - Devices that lose settings or take a long time to restart
  - Anything with a safety function

Tips
  - Use plugs with energy monitoring to find the real standby draw before
    deciding what to cut.
  - "Master and controlled" power strips switch peripherals off when the main
    device (like a TV or computer) is off.
  - Do not plug heating appliances such as space heaters into ordinary smart
    plugs unless the plug is rated for that load.
  - Check the plug's amperage rating against the device.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WATER HEATING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Water heating is often one of the largest energy uses after space
conditioning.

  - Set the heater thermostat to about 120 F (49 C) unless a specific need
    requires higher.
  - Use schedules or smart controllers on electric tank heaters so they heat
    water at low-cost times and reduce standby loss during long absences.
  - Heat pump water heaters use much less electricity than standard electric
    resistance heaters. Many include scheduling and demand response modes.
  - Recirculation pumps should run on a timer, motion sensor or demand button,
    not continuously.
  - Install leak sensors near water heaters, washing machines, sinks and
    under dishwashers. Consider an automatic shutoff valve.
  - Insulate hot water pipes and tank where practical.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. APPLIANCES, EV CHARGING AND OTHER LARGE LOADS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Use delayed-start features on dishwashers, washers and dryers to run
    during off-peak periods on time-of-use rates.
  - Run dryers when not on the critical peak, and clean lint filters and
    vents regularly.
  - Schedule EV charging for off-peak hours or for midday if you have solar.
    Many chargers and vehicles have built-in scheduling.
  - Run pool pumps for the minimum hours needed for filtration, during
    off-peak hours. Variable-speed pumps save substantially compared with
    single-speed pumps.
  - Use smart plugs or controllers for dehumidifiers, sump pumps (with
    care), and garage or workshop equipment.
  - Check refrigerator door alarms and temperature alerts to prevent waste
    and spoiled food.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. ENERGY MONITORING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Monitoring tells you what to automate and proves whether it worked.

  Level 1  Utility portal / smart meter data: daily or hourly usage.
  Level 2  Whole-home energy monitor: clamps on the main service for
           real-time usage.
  Level 3  Circuit-level monitor or smart panel: shows usage by circuit
           (HVAC, water heater, EV charger, dryer).
  Level 4  Plug-level monitoring: individual device usage.

Tips
  - Record a baseline for at least two weeks before making changes.
  - Look for the overnight base load (usage while everyone is asleep). A high
    base load points to standby loads or equipment running when it should not.
  - Set alerts for unusual spikes, for example a dryer running for hours or an
    EV charging at peak.
  - Review monthly and compare to weather, not just to last month.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. TIME-OF-USE RATES AND DEMAND RESPONSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Time-of-use (TOU) rates charge more at peak times. Shift flexible loads
    (EV charging, water heating, laundry, dishwasher, pool pump) to the
    cheapest periods.
  - Pre-cooling: cool the home slightly below normal before the peak window,
    then let it drift up during the peak.
  - Utility demand response programs reward households that allow short
    adjustments to thermostats, water heaters or EV chargers during grid
    stress. Check participation rules, overrides and what the utility can
    control.
  - Keep manual override options. Comfort and safety must come first.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. WINDOW COVERINGS AND SHADING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Motorized shades can close automatically when direct sun hits a window in
    cooling season and open in winter to capture solar heat.
  - Use sun-position or temperature-based triggers instead of only a clock.
  - In winter, close insulating shades at sunset to reduce heat loss.
  - Check that the automation does not block daylight when you want to avoid
    turning on lights.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. EXAMPLE AUTOMATION RULES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. When the last person leaves (geofence), set the thermostat to away mode
     and turn off all lights and entertainment plugs.
  2. When the first person arrives home, start recovery 20 to 30 minutes
     before arrival, based on your system's recovery time.
  3. At bedtime, turn off common-area lights and entertainment plugs and set
     the nighttime thermostat schedule.
  4. When a window or door has been open for 5 minutes, pause heating and
     cooling and send a notification.
  5. In the closet, bathroom and laundry room, turn lights off 5 minutes after
     motion stops.
  6. Delay dishwasher and washer start until off-peak hours.
  7. Schedule EV charging between the cheapest hours.
  8. When midday solar production exceeds a threshold, start flexible loads
     such as the water heater, pool pump or EV charger.
  9. If the garage door has been open for 10 minutes, send an alert.
  10. If an unusual power spike lasts more than 2 hours, send an alert.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. PLATFORMS, PROTOCOLS AND INTEROPERABILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Matter is a common standard intended to let devices from different
    brands work together across major ecosystems.
  - Thread, Zigbee and Z-Wave are low-power mesh protocols used by sensors
    and switches. Wi-Fi is common for higher-bandwidth devices and is more
    power hungry.
  - Local control (automations that run on a hub in your home) keeps
    working if the internet fails and is generally faster and more private
    than cloud-only control.
  - Check that your devices support the ecosystem you chose and that the
    manufacturer provides long-term updates.
  - Avoid mixing many separate apps. Use one primary platform for
    automations so rules do not conflict.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. SECURITY, PRIVACY AND RELIABILITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Use strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication on accounts.
  - Keep device firmware updated.
  - Put smart devices on a separate guest or IoT network where possible.
  - Review what data is collected and shared, and the vendor's retention
    policy. Occupancy data can reveal when no one is home.
  - Provide manual controls for every automated system. A light switch must
    still work if the hub is down.
  - Test the "internet down" scenario by unplugging the router briefly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
14. COMMON PITFALLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Buying devices before measuring where energy goes
  - Counting the energy cost of the smart devices themselves. Hubs, cameras,
    and bulbs draw power continuously; typical small devices use a watt or
    two, which adds up with dozens of devices.
  - Too many conflicting schedules in different apps
  - Automations that override occupants without an easy manual override
  - Putting hubs and routers on plugs that get turned off
  - Ignoring firmware support. Devices that stop receiving updates become
    security and compatibility risks.
  - Overusing aggressive thermostat setbacks with heat pumps
  - Relying on cloud-only control for critical functions
